Catching neutrinos: guess first, then see

Neutrinos are tiny particles that pass through almost anything, including the whole Earth. The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Francis Halzen for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. IceCube watches a cubic kilometre of deep Antarctic ice with 5,160 light sensors.

Why so big, and why so many sensors? Three questions. For each one, make a guess first, then reveal what our small computer models say. They are toy models: they show the trend, not IceCube's real numbers. Everything was worked out in advance; nothing is computed live and nothing is sent anywhere.

Step 1 of 4: the odds

If a high-energy neutrino crosses a kilometre of ice, what are the odds it hits anything?

Take one neutrino with an energy of 100 TeV, the kind IceCube hunts. It flies straight through 1 km of ice. To leave a trace it must hit an atomic nucleus on the way.

Step 2 of 4: how big a detector?

How many cosmic neutrinos does a block of ice catch in a year?

Neutrinos from space arrive all the time, from every direction, but very few interact. A bigger block of ice holds more targets. Picture a cube of ice 1 km on each side.

Your guess: cosmic neutrinos caught per year in a 1 km cube

Pick one first.

The Earth as a shield

IceCube also uses the Earth as a filter: neutrinos coming up from below have crossed the planet. At high energy, the Earth starts to stop them.

Your guess: of 1 PeV neutrinos coming straight up through the whole Earth, how many get through?

Pick one first.

Step 3 of 4: build a telescope

How far apart can the strings of sensors be?

A muon made by a neutrino crosses the ice and gives off a cone of blue light. The sensors record when the light arrives, and a fit turns those times into a direction on the sky. IceCube's strings of sensors stand about 125 m apart. Spread them twice as far over the same patch of ice (a quarter as many strings) and fewer sensors see the light.

Your guess: at 250 m instead of 125 m (same patch, 1 TeV muons), how much worse does the aim get?

Pick one first.

What is real and what is a toy

Real (taken from published sources, listed in each experiment's README):

  • Neutrino cross sections from published tables (the nuFATE tables, MIT licence) and a fitted model of the Earth's density (from nuFATE).
  • The size of the cosmic neutrino flux and its spectrum, from an IceCube measurement (the 7.5-year high-energy starting-event fit).
  • The positions of IceCube's 5,160 in-ice sensors (from the public ice-model archive), the Cherenkov angle and the Frank–Tamm light yield.

Toy (our simplifications):

  • Steps 1 and 2 count every neutrino interaction in a block of ice (step 2: above 60 TeV of neutrino energy, one power-law flux). No light, sensors, trigger or selection, and no atmospheric neutrinos.
  • Through the Earth: straight-line absorption only. Neutrinos that scatter and carry on with less energy are not followed, and the Glashow resonance is left out.
  • The telescope: one-energy muons on straight lines, uniform ice (no dust layer, no direction dependence), no noise hits, one arrival time per sensor, and a fit that uses exactly the light-delay model the simulation used, which flatters it. One light constant is tuned, so its angles show the trend, not IceCube's performance.
  • The full list of simplifications is in each experiment's README.
