**What the committee credits to each laureate:**

- **Hegemann.** Asked how *Chlamydomonas* reacts to light so fast. In the early 1990s he proposed that a single protein complex both captures light and acts as an ion channel, an idea first met with scepticism ([POP] pp.2-3). His team recorded the alga's light-triggered currents with a suction electrode (Harz & Hegemann 1991; [SCI] p.4). Note that the two committee documents describe the delay differently: [SCI] p.4 says "only microseconds after flash stimulation", while [POP] p.2 says "just half a millisecond after light reached the eyespot". His lab identified the channelopsin-1 and -2 sequences (GenBank AF385748, AF461397) ([SCI] pp.4-5) and contacted Nagel to test the genes ([POP] p.3).
- **Nagel.** Expressed these genes in frog (*Xenopus*) eggs and recorded them electrically ([POP] p.3).
  - 2002: showed that ChR1 is a light-gated channel that mostly carries protons (Nagel et al. 2002, *Science*).
  - 2003: showed that ChR2 is a directly light-gated channel for positive ions. It works in oocytes and in mammalian cells, which became light-sensitive (Nagel et al. 2003, *PNAS*).
  - The committee calls the 2003 result "arguably more consequential" ([SCI] p.6).
  - Nagel led the work, with Hegemann and Ernst Bamberg ([SCI] p.5).
- **Deisseroth.** His lab gave the first demonstration that ChR2 can be expressed in neurons and used to fire them with blue light at millisecond precision (Boyden et al. 2005, *Nat Neurosci*). He used a construct that Nagel provided ([SCI] p.6). In 2007 his group used optical fibres to control neurons in the brains of living mice (Aravanis et al. 2007, *J Neural Eng*), moving the whiskers ([SCI] pp.7-8; [POP] p.6). The press release puts it this way: Deisseroth "transformed the protein into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells" ([PR]).

The five key publications listed by the committee ([POP] p.8):

1. Harz, Nonnengässer & Hegemann 1992, *Phil Trans R Soc B* 338:39-52
2. Nagel et al. 2002, *Science* 296:2395-2398. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12089443/> (doi:10.1126/science.1072068)
3. Nagel et al. 2003, *PNAS* 100:13940-13945. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14615590/> (doi:10.1073/pnas.1936192100)
4. Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel & Deisseroth 2005, *Nat Neurosci* 8:1263-1268. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16116447/> (doi:10.1038/nn1525)
5. Aravanis et al. 2007, *J Neural Eng* 4:S143-156. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17873414/>

### Other contributors, and how the sources describe credit

This section lists what the sources say about other people involved. It is a record of what is written, not a ranking and not a judgement.

**Authorship of the founding papers**

- The authors of the 2005 paper are Edward S. Boyden (first author), Feng Zhang, Ernst Bamberg, Georg Nagel and Karl Deisseroth (last author): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16116447/>
- Ernst Bamberg also co-authored the 2002 and 2003 channelrhodopsin papers: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12089443/>, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14615590/>
- The committee identifies Bamberg as Nagel's PhD supervisor at the MPI of Biophysics and a collaborator on both papers ([SCI] p.5).

**How the official texts attribute the 2005 work**

- The press release says Deisseroth introduced the gene into rat nerve cells and "published this breakthrough in 2005" ([PR]).
- The scientific background attributes the result to "the Karl Deisseroth laboratory" and cites Boyden's own historical accounts (Boyden 2011a,b) for how the work happened ([SCI] p.6).

**Other people named in the scientific background (none are laureates)**

- **Feng Zhang:** led the 2007 halorhodopsin silencing paper in Deisseroth's lab ([SCI] p.8).
- **Xue Han and Ed Boyden:** an independent 2007 halorhodopsin study ([SCI] p.8).
- **Gero Miesenböck:** the first genetic light-sensitisation of neurons (chARGe, 2002), and one of the six co-authors of the 2006 review that coined the term "optogenetics" ([SCI] pp.2, 7).
- **John Spudich's and Takahashi's groups:** identified the same algal genes independently ([SCI] pp.4-5).
- **Zhuo-Hua Pan's lab:** vision restoration in mice (Bi et al. 2006) ([SCI] p.10).
- **Herlitze/Landmesser (Li et al. 2005) and Yawo (Ishizuka et al. 2006):** early independent studies of ChR2 in neurons ([SCI] p.7).

**Boyden's own account** (Boyden 2011, *F1000 Biol Rep* 3:11): <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155186/>

- At the time he was a PhD student in the labs of Jennifer Raymond and Richard Tsien, and Deisseroth was a postdoc in Robert Malenka's lab.
- In February 2004 Boyden proposed contacting Nagel. Deisseroth emailed Nagel in March 2004 and got the ChR2 clone on a collaborative basis.
- Around 1 a.m. on 4 August 2004, Boyden recorded the first ChR2 neuron, in the Tsien lab. It fired on blue light.
- Deisseroth became an assistant professor in January 2005. Feng Zhang was then a first-year chemistry graduate student.
- Boyden writes that he regrets the first paper did not acknowledge that many key experiments were done in Tsien's lab.

**Deisseroth's own account** (Deisseroth 2015, *Nat Neurosci* 18:1213): <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790845/>

- He thanks Nagel "for promptly sending the initial channelrhodopsin clone in response to my e-mail request", and thanks Boyden and Zhang for the 2005 paper.
- The popular background similarly says Deisseroth wrote to Nagel to ask for the DNA ([POP] p.5).

**Coverage of the credit question**

- STAT describes Boyden as "a Ph.D. student in his [Deisseroth's] lab". Boyden's 2011 account above says he was doing his PhD in the labs of Raymond and Tsien. <https://www.statnews.com/2026/10/05/nobel-prize-medicine-2026-winner-deisseroth-hegemann-nagel/>
- STAT reports that many social-media commentators said Boyden should not have been left out, and that Boyden (now at MIT) did not respond to requests for comment (same STAT URL).
- The prize can go to at most three people. Sources: STAT (same URL), which writes that the Nobel rules limit the award to no more than three, and Nature's Ewen Callaway, who writes that it is "limited to three awardees": <https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03094-z>
- The 2013 Brain Prize went to six people "for their invention and refinement of optogenetics": Bamberg, Boyden, Deisseroth, Hegemann, Miesenböck and Nagel. <https://brainprize.org/winners/optogenetics-2013>
- The 2021 Lasker Basic Medical Research Award went to Deisseroth, Hegemann and Dieter Oesterhelt (the bacteriorhodopsin discoverer): <https://laskerfoundation.org/winners/light-sensitive-microbial-proteins-optogenetics/> and <https://www.jci.org/articles/view/154418>

## 4. How channelrhodopsin works (plain language)

1. Channelrhodopsin is a protein with seven membrane-spanning segments. It sits in the cell membrane and holds a light-catching molecule, **all-trans retinal**, which is derived from vitamin A. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16360690/> ; <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155186/>
2. When retinal absorbs a photon it changes shape (isomerizes). This makes the protein open a pore through itself, so the light sensor and the channel are the same molecule. No chain of messenger molecules is needed. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC283525/> (Nagel et al. 2003 suggest that the channel opens "after a light-induced isomerization of all-trans retinal"); [SCI] p.6
3. ChR2 is a **non-selective cation channel**. Positive ions (H+, Na+, K+, Ca2+) flow passively down their gradient, mostly inward, which **depolarizes** the cell and can trigger an action potential. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14615590/> ; [SCI] p.6
4. It is fast. In frog eggs the channel opened within about 0.2 ms of a light pulse ([POP] p.4), and in neurons it controls spiking with **millisecond** precision, down to single spikes. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16116447/>
5. ChR2 responds to **blue light**. Its action spectrum peaks at about 460 nm (<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC283525/>), and in vivo work typically uses about 470 nm, for example the 473 nm laser in Aravanis et al. 2007 (<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17873414/>).
6. Mammalian tissue already contains enough retinal, so only the gene has to be delivered (for example with a virus) into chosen cell types. The committee calls Nagel's observation that no added retinal was needed "fortuitous", and no added retinal was needed in living animals either. [SCI] pp.6-8 ; <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17873414/>
7. **Red-shifted** activators allow light of a longer wavelength to be used. Chrimson peaks at 590 nm (<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3943671/>). ChRmine is red-shifted and extremely light-sensitive, and was later engineered into rsChRmine and other variants (<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35114111/>).
8. **Silencers** do the opposite:
   - Halorhodopsin (NpHR) pumps chloride into the cell under yellow light, hyperpolarizing it, but it moves only one ion per absorbed photon ([SCI] p.8).
   - GtACR anion channels let chloride flow passively. They silence neurons at less than one-thousandth of the light needed by the most efficient earlier tools. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26113638/>

## 5. Ten facts worth knowing

1. **Francis Crick speculated about it.** In 1999 the DNA co-discoverer wrote that to switch neuron types on and off in a living animal "the ideal signal would be light", and acknowledged the idea was "rather far-fetched" (as quoted in [SCI] p.2). <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10670022/>
2. **It started with pond algae.** *Chlamydomonas* is about 0.015 mm across and swims toward light, which it senses with a tiny orange "eyespot". A dish of it visibly shifts colour toward a lit side. [POP] p.2
3. **The alga's response is more than twenty times faster than our eyes'.** The alga's electrical response comes half a millisecond after light reaches the eyespot, while light reaching a human eye triggers a chemical chain that takes at least 10 ms. [POP] pp.2-3
4. **Frog eggs cracked it.** Nagel injected the algal genes into *Xenopus* frog eggs, which then made the proteins in their membranes, and recorded the light-triggered currents. [POP] p.3 ; <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12089443/>
5. **Neurons already had the missing ingredient.** An added light-catching cofactor might have been needed. It turned out trace levels already present in mammalian neurons were enough, and no exogenous retinal was required in living animals. [SCI] pp.6-8 ; <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17873414/>
6. **The 2003 paper pointed the way.** Nagel, Hegemann, Bamberg et al. wrote that ChR2 "should become a useful tool" to control membrane potential, especially in mammalian cells, two years before it was used in neurons. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC283525/>
7. **The first neuron worked on the first try, at 1 a.m.** On 4 August 2004 the very first ChR2 neuron Boyden patched fired precise action potentials to blue light. His email to Deisseroth read "Tired, but excited." <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155186/>
8. **Researchers have created a false memory in mice.** By re-activating light-tagged hippocampal cells during a shock in another place, the Tonegawa lab made mice freeze in a room where no shock had been given. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23888038/>
9. **The laureate still treats patients.** Deisseroth is a practicing psychiatrist who sees people with treatment-resistant depression and autism spectrum disorders. He also co-developed CLARITY, a way to make brain tissue transparent (2013). <https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/10/deisseroth-nobel-prize.html> ; <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23575631/>
10. **A 2005 co-author went on to work on CRISPR.** Feng Zhang, a first-year graduate student when the 2005 paper was written, was a senior author of the 2013 paper that engineered CRISPR/Cas systems for genome editing in human and mouse cells. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3155186/> ; <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23287718/>

More facts:

- **Waking mice with light (2007).** Light on hypocretin neurons increased the chance that sleeping mice woke. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17943086/>
- **Heart and anxiety.** Optically speeding up a mouse's heart enhanced anxiety-like behaviour in risky contexts. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859543/> ; [POP] p.7
- **Light through the skull.** ChRmine has driven deep-brain circuits through the intact skull, as deep as 7 mm. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33020604/>
- **A long wait.** Nature's reporter writes that scientists had anticipated an optogenetics prize for years, and neuroscientist Michael Häusser asked "What took them so long?" <https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03094-z>
- **An earlier halorhodopsin link.** Hegemann co-authored a 1985 paper with Dieter Oesterhelt on the photocycle of halorhodopsin, the protein later used to silence neurons. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15938053/>

## 6. Open questions and limits stated by the field

- **Light delivery depth.** "Visible light cannot penetrate deep inside brain tissue" (Chen et al. 2018, *Science*): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29439241/>
  - Deisseroth describes the need for an interface that delivers about 100 mW per square millimetre at its output, "~100× greater than needed at the opsin-expressing cells themselves because of expected scattering losses". He calls controlling every cell of a whole brain individually "not likely to be realizable", in part because of light scattering and power requirements. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790845/>
  - Workarounds being tested: red-shifted opsins (<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33020604/>), upconversion nanoparticles that turn near-infrared into visible light (<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29439241/>), and implanted fibres or LEDs.
- **Heating.** Commonly used illumination protocols raised brain temperature by 0.2-2 °C and suppressed spiking in multiple brain regions, which biased behaviour in mice (Owen, Liu & Kreitzer 2019): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31209378/>. High-intensity fibre light can raise local firing without any opsin present (Stujenske et al. 2015): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26166563/>. No-opsin control groups are standard practice for this reason: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790845/>
- **Gene delivery and immune response.** Every therapeutic use needs gene therapy, usually AAV. In PIONEER about 70% of patients had mild intraocular inflammation that resolved after corticosteroid treatment: <https://www.cgtlive.com/view/gene-therapy-continues-to-be-well-tolerated-in-retinitis-pigmentosa>. Early on, neurons were known to be "highly vulnerable" to overexpressed foreign membrane proteins, and sensitive to heat and light: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790845/>
- **Inhibitory tools can misbehave.** At axon terminals, chloride-conducting channelrhodopsins triggered transmitter release when the light came on, and sustained archaerhodopsin increased spontaneous release (Mahn et al. 2016): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26950004/>. Inhibitory experiments face "unique challenges" in design and interpretation (Wiegert et al. 2017): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28772120/>
- **Clinical translation is early.**
  - The 2021 vision result was a single patient. The patient perceived objects only with the vector-treated eye while wearing the goggles, and could not detect objects before injection. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34031601/>
  - The committee notes that several retinitis pigmentosa trials are "presently underway" ([SCI] p.10).
  - Optical cochlear implants are still in rodent studies: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32718992/> ; [POP] p.7
  - The committee points to chemogenetics (DREADDs) as a complementary tool that "may eventually be used in the human brain to counteract various diseases" ([SCI] p.11).
- **A slow start.** For roughly 2005-2009 there were "difficulties in implementation, few publications and limited biological findings" (Deisseroth 2015): <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26308982/>
