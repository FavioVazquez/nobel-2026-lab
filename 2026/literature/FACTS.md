# Nobel Prize in Literature 2026: Anne Carson fact sheet

> Educational demos made to show an open-source tool. Not research.

This is the sourced fact sheet behind the Literature folder. Every statement names where it comes from. It was
compiled on 2026-10-08, the day of the announcement, from the official Nobel pages (the press release in English and
Swedish, the summary and facts pages, and the Nobel Committee's bio-bibliography), the Nobel Prize API, and, for the
ancient authors, the Suda On Line, Grenfell and Hunt's 1914 edition of the Oxyrhynchus papyri and the other sources
listed in the [survival ledger](survival-ledger/README.md). The sentences we actually use in the video, the page and the
READMEs are listed one by one, with a check date, in [CLAIMS.md](CLAIMS.md). The ids in brackets (L01, L02, ...) point
to that ledger.

**She is a living author.** No passage of her work appears in this folder. Her own words appear only as the one short
phrase the Nobel Committee itself quotes (section 4, marked, L09) and a few short quotations used as commentary, each
at most 15 words, attributed, with the pages that confirm the wording (section 9, L59, L60, L62; and for the video L63, L64, L65). No description of her appearance, no portrait (the
Nobel illustration stays off too), no cover art. Facts about her books (titles, subtitles, years, publishers, and what
the Committee says they are) are used; titles are facts.

Toy results and our own counts are not facts about her and live in the experiment folders, each with its own label.

### Her place in the list (counted from the Nobel API)

- **The 19th woman** to receive the Literature prize: API laureates with `gender` "female" give 19 records, Lagerlöf
  1909 ... Carson 2026. nobelprize.org's list of women laureates shows the same 19 Literature entries. [API], [WOMEN] (L18)
- **1901-2026:** 126 award years; no award in 7 (1914, 1918, 1935, 1940, 1941, 1942, 1943), so 119 prizes; 4 shared
  (1904, 1917, 1966, 1974), so 123 laureates. [API] (L20)
- **By decade:** from 1950 to 1989 the only woman laureate was Nelly Sachs (1966); in the 2020s so far (2020-2026, 7
  prizes) 4 of 7 laureates are women. [API] (L21). Full table: [forms/README.md](forms/README.md#3-the-prizes-own-history-nobel-api-cc0).
- **Canadians: say which field.** The Academy calls two laureates "the Canadian author": Alice Munro (2013) and Anne
  Carson (2026). Three Literature laureates were born in Canada by the API's birth-country field: Saul Bellow (1976),
  Munro and Carson. Bellow was born in Lachine, Quebec, grew up in Chicago, and his 1976 press release places him in
  "American narrative art". Safe sentence: the second Canadian Literature laureate, after Alice Munro (2013). Not "the
  third Canadian". [PR], [PR13], [PR76], [API] (L19, L43)

## 3. What the Committee says about her books

All from [BIO]. The Committee calls its list "Bibliography – a selection" (L22). Years and titles as printed there.

- ***Eros the Bittersweet: An Essay*** (1986): "lays the foundation for her subsequent authorship"; it "draws heavily
  on a compound adjective from Sappho that defines Eros as pain and sweetness combined". (L08)
- ***Economy of the Unlost*** (1999) sets the sixth-century BC Greek poet Simonides of Keos against Paul Celan; ***Men
  in the Off Hours*** (2000) aligns the historian Thucydides with Virginia Woolf. (L10)
- **"The Glass Essay"** in ***Glass, Irony, and God*** (1995) "demonstrates the potential of the long poem where genre
  boundaries are seen to blur". (L11)
- ***Autobiography of Red: A Novel in Verse*** (1998) takes as its starting point "poem fragments by the sixth century
  BC Greek poet Stesichoros concerning the winged red monster Geryon". The Committee says it is "often heralded as
  Carson's greatest": its words, never ours. (L12, L45)
- ***The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos*** (2001): twenty-nine episodes. (L13)
- ***Nox*** (2010): "an epitaph in the form of a box containing an accordion-fold-out of pictures, letters and other
  written texts", "a creation as sculptural as it is literary"; Catullus' elegy 101 and her translation of it are part
  of it. (L14)
- ***Float*** (2016): "twenty-two chapbooks on different subjects, capable of being read in any order". (L15)
- ***Wrong Norma*** (2024): "a diverse combination of texts with a strongly performative aspect". (L16)
- Translations "may be regarded as independent works in their own right": *If Not, Winter* (Sappho, 2002), Sophocles'
  *Electra* (2001) and *Antigone* (2015), Euripides' *Bakkhai* (2015). (L07)
- Collaborations "with visual artists and choreographers and with opera and theatre directors"; the bibliography lists
  libretti (*The Mirror of Simple Souls: An Opera Installation*, 2003; *The Mile-Long Opera*, 2018), a text "for
  Narrator and Orchestra" (*Troyjam*, 2008) and a comic (*The Trojan Women: A Comic*, 2021). (L17)

The forms named on each of the 49 entries, with the quoted evidence for each tag, are counted in
[forms/README.md](forms/README.md) (L23, L24).

## 5. The classical tradition she answers: what survives

All from [LEDGER], each with its sources there. Ancient counts are testimony (the Suda is a 10th-century Byzantine
encyclopedia), modern counts are estimates, and they disagree; we give ranges and never one "percentage lost" (L39, L40).

- **Sophocles:** 7 complete plays of more than 120; the Suda says 123. (L27)
- **Euripides:** 18 or 19 plays of about 90; the Suda says 75 or 92. (L28)
- **Aeschylus:** 7 plays, one of disputed authorship, of 70 to 90; the Suda says 90. (L29)
- **Stesichoros:** 26 books (Suda); only fragments survive. His Geryon poem had at least 1,300 lines (a line number in
  a papyrus margin); about 180 are readable. (L30, L31)
- **Sappho:** about 9,000 to 10,000 lines in all (estimates); about 650 survive; Book I alone held 1,320 verses, the
  number written on the papyrus roll's own end-title; only fragment 1 (the Ode to Aphrodite) is certainly complete.
  (L32-L35)
- **Catullus:** about 113 poems, which reached us (all but one poem) through one manuscript known at Verona around
  1300 and lost before 1400. (L36)
- **Simonides:** fragments; a papyrus published in 1992 added much. (L37)
- **Thucydides:** all 8 books, but the history breaks off mid-sentence in 411 BC. (L38)

## 8. How Sappho survives, and her translators (the fragments and translators stations)

All Greek, Latin and English shown is public domain, stored with its page or fragment anchor in
[fragments/](fragments/README.md) and [translators/](translators/README.md). Numbers marked "our count" are ours, by a
stated rule, over a 1908 or 1914 edition: they describe that edition, not Sappho's surviving work today.

- **A torn papyrus.** P.Oxy. 1231, a roll copied in the 2nd century AD and found at Oxyrhynchus, published by
  Grenfell and Hunt in 1914, holds the poem now numbered Sappho 16 (Voigt). In their text of 22 lines, 95 of 483
  printed letters are restorations in square brackets and 4 more were added where the scribe left them out: about one
  letter in five is the editors' (our count). (L47, L48)
- **A quotation that stops.** Sappho 31 survives because "Longinus" quoted it in *On the Sublime*; the quotation stops
  after the first line of a fifth stanza (Rhys Roberts 1899, p. 70). (L49)
- **Fragment 1**, the Ode to Aphrodite, is quoted by Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Wharton's note). (L50)
- **Fragments of a few words.** Of the 126 fragments Wharton (1908) prints as Greek verse, the median has 7 Greek
  words and 45, about a third, have 5 or fewer (our count). Not "half". (L51, L52)
- **Who saved them.** As printed by Wharton: grammarians and dictionaries, metricians, anthologies and critics; most
  of all Hephaestion (23 fragments), Athenaeus (20), Apollonius Dyscolus (17). (L53)
- **Numbering.** Wharton follows Bergk; modern books follow Voigt (1971): Wharton 2 = Voigt 31, Wharton 40 = Voigt 130. (L54)
- **Six versions of Sappho 31**, all printed in Wharton (1908): Catullus 51 (1st century BC), Ambrose Philips 1711,
  Tobias Smollett 1748, J. H. Merivale 1833, J. A. Symonds 1883, Wharton's prose 1885. (L55)
- **"Seems to me."** Kept by Catullus, Symonds and Wharton; Philips and Merivale open "Blest as the immortal gods is
  he". First stanza (16 Greek words) carried, our rough count, not a quality score: Catullus 13, Philips 13,
  Smollett 0, Merivale 11, Symonds 15, Wharton 15. (L56, L57)
- **γλυκύπικρον** (Wharton fr. 40 = Voigt 130): γλυκύς "sweet" + πικρός "bitter" (LSJ); Wharton and Symonds both write
  "bitter-sweet", order reversed. The Committee says *Eros the Bittersweet* draws on "a compound adjective from
  Sappho" without naming it. (L58, L08)
