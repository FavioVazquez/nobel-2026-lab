# Insights (can you predict spending?)

> **Educational demos made to show an open-source tool. Not research.** Each sentence names the field of
> `results/spending.json` it comes from; a test checks the numbers.

1. Hall's benchmark is zero: if households spend on what they expect to earn over their lives, nothing known in advance predicts next quarter's change in their spending; in US data from 1953 to 2019, spending and income growth from two to four quarters back predict 11.5 per cent of the changes in spending on non-durables and services, and 2.9 per cent when the years up to 2026 are included. (fields: `guess.a.hall_benchmark_pct`, `guess.a.quarters`, `guess.a.answer_pct`, `guess.a.full_sample_pct`, `guess.a.full_sample_quarters`)
2. Add the most recent quarter and the share rises to 22.0 per cent, but part of that comes from how the data are measured: quarterly figures are averages, and averaging makes even an unpredictable path look predictable from the quarter before (Working 1960). (fields: `guess.a.secondary_lags_1_4_pct`, `guess.a.why_lags_2_4`)
3. The published benchmarks: Campbell and Mankiw estimated that about 50 per cent of income goes to households who spend as they earn, in US data from 1953 to 1986, with estimates from 0.351 to 0.698 in their main table; Hall and Mishkin, as the committee's scientific background states it, put the hand-to-mouth fraction at 20 per cent of consumption, in the food spending of US families from 1969 to 1975. (fields: `guess.b.published`)
4. On our data from 1953 to 2019 that share is very uncertain: the 95 per cent interval runs from 27.9 to 73.9 per cent, the effective first-stage F is 4.86, below the rule of thumb of 10, and a method built for weak instruments gives 55.8 to 103.1 per cent, a range that leaves out the usual estimate and runs above 100 per cent, which a share cannot be: a sign that the simple two-group model does not fit these data well. (fields: `guess.b.ours`, `sample.ci_level_pct`)
5. A simulated control shows the problem: when the past barely predicts income, the usual 95 per cent interval holds the true share in only 55.1 to 57.5 per cent of 1000 runs, while the weak-instrument method holds it in 88.9 to 91.8 per cent, about as often as when the instruments are strong (median effective F 31.1), though below the nominal 95 because its test rejects too often in samples this size. (fields: `sample.ci_level_pct`, `control.cases.random_walk_weak.ci95_coverage_pct`, `control.cases.hand_to_mouth_weak.ci95_coverage_pct`, `control.params.reps`, `control.cases.random_walk_weak.ar_coverage_pct`, `control.cases.hand_to_mouth_weak.ar_coverage_pct`, `control.cases.hand_to_mouth.median_effective_F`)
