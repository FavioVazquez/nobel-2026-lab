# Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026: Kagan and Soai fact sheet

> Educational demos made to show an open-source tool. Not research.

This is the sourced fact sheet behind the Chemistry folder. Every statement names where it comes from. It was compiled on 2026-10-07, the day of the announcement, from the official Nobel documents (the press release, the summary and facts pages, the popular-science PDF and the 22-page scientific background PDF), from the laureates' own papers (read as abstracts on PubMed, Crossref or the publisher's page), from Kenso Soai's open-access 2019 review, his 2011 review with Tsuneomi Kawasaki and his 2013 lecture slides, and from two papers by Daniel Singleton and Lam Vo. The claims we actually use in videos and READMEs are listed one by one, with a check date, in [CLAIMS.md](CLAIMS.md). The ids in brackets below (C01, C02, ...) point to that ledger.

Toy-model results are not facts and do not belong in this file or in the ledger. They live in the experiment folders, each with its own label.

This file stays at textbook level on purpose. It names no lab procedure, no amount, no condition and no way of handling any chemical. The Soai reaction uses reactive zinc reagents; we never describe how to run it.

## 1. Citation and date

- **Citation:** "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis" ([PR], [SUM], [KF], [SF]) (C01)
- **Announced:** 7 October 2026 by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences ([PR]). (C01)
- **Share:** 1/2 each ([SUM], [KF], [SF]). The prize is 12 million Swedish kronor, "to be shared equally between the laureates" ([PR]). (C02)
- **Press-release headline:** "They made chemistry choose a mirror image" ([PR])
- **Committee chair quote:** they "have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously" (Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, [PR]) (C05)
- **The committee's own wording of the award** is slightly different: "for discovering non-linear effects in asymmetric catalysis and asymmetric autocatalysis" ([SCI] p. 2). We quote the citation, never this line, as the prize motivation.
- **Illustrations:** by Johan Jarnestad, "free to use for non-commercial purposes" with attribution ([PR]). We do not reuse them.

| Training | PhD 1960, Collège de France ([PR]) | PhD 1979, University of Tokyo ([PR]) |

**What the committee credits to Kagan:**

- **The 1986 discovery.** The product of a reaction can have a bigger lead of one hand (a higher ee) than the one-handed helper that made it, "a situation that was previously unthinkable" ([SCI] p. 2). The press release: "Henri Kagan took the first decisive step in 1986" ([PR]). (C12)
- **The explanation.** A metal can hold two chiral ligands, so a ligand that is a mix of both hands gives three catalysts, and the mixed one behaves differently ([SCI] pp. 6-7; [POP] p. 4). (C14)
- **The idea came early.** "Kagan had already contemplated this possibility in the late 1960s" ([SCI] p. 6). In 1994 his group extended the model to three and four ligands and to the "reservoir effect" ([SCI] p. 7). (C36)
- **Not in the Nobel texts:** Kagan's other well-known work (the DIOP ligand, samarium iodide). None of the Nobel documents mentions it, so neither do we.

**What the committee credits to Soai:**

- **1990: the first asymmetric autocatalysis**, but the copy had a smaller lead of one hand than the original (86% ee in, 35% ee out) ([SCI] pp. 4, 11). (C18)
- **1995: autocatalysis with amplification**, the first reaction to meet all three of Frank's criteria ([SCI] p. 4; [S95]). (C19)
- **2003: from a lead of almost nothing to more than 99.5% ee**, and a hand chosen by chance when no chiral substance is added ([SCI] pp. 12-13; [S03a]; [S03b]). (C21, C24)
- **The reaction bears his name:** "the Soai reaction" ([SCI] pp. 2, 12). (C37)

## 3. The story in 15 lines

1. On 7 October 2026 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences gave the Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai, "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis". (C01, C02)
2. Some molecules come as two mirror images, like left and right hands. (C06)
3. Life uses only one hand: of each amino-acid pair, only one is found in the proteins in your cells, and life uses only one mirror image of the sugars in DNA. Chemists call this homochiral, from the Greek for "same" and "hand". (C07, C08)
4. Left to itself, a reaction that can make both hands gives equal amounts of each. A one-handed (chiral) catalyst can favour one hand. (C09, C10)
5. Before 1986, chemists assumed a straight line: a catalyst with a 50% lead of one hand (50% ee) gives a product with a 50% lead. (C11)
6. In 1986 Kagan showed the line can bend. In three reactions, the product's lead of one hand (ee) was not proportional to the catalyst's: one bent up, two bent down. (C12, C13)
7. His explanation: a metal can hold two one-handed molecules, so a mix gives right-right, left-left and left-right catalysts, and the mixed one barely works. (C14, C16)
8. The Nobel's own example: start at 75:25, get catalysts of 56%, 38% and 6%, and the working catalysts are 90:10. (C15)
9. A copier alone is not enough: a copy that is 90% faithful loses some of its lead of one hand at every round (100%, 90%, 81% ee ...). The copy must also hold back its mirror image. (C17)
10. Soai built the copier. His first one (1990) lost some of its lead of one hand. In 1995 he reported, in Nature, a molecule that makes more of itself and whose lead of one hand grows as it goes. (C18, C19)
11. In 2003 his group started from a catalyst with an excess of about 0.00005%, five parts in ten million, and reached 57%, then 99%, then more than 99.5% in three runs. (C21, C22)
12. With no chiral substance added, the reaction still picks a hand, at random: 19 runs gave one hand and 18 the other. An independent lab got 27 and 27 in 54 runs. (C24, C25)
13. This is the coin flip that Charles Frank, a theoretical physicist, described in 1953: self-copying plus mutual antagonism can turn a tiny chance imbalance into one hand. (C27)
14. The committee calls the Soai reaction "an important proof of concept", but says Frank's model "is not an answer to the origin of biological homochirality". Two different detailed mechanisms have been proposed for the reaction. (C28, C29, C30)
15. Why it matters: for many drugs one mirror form works and the other "can cause unnecessary and sometimes harmful side effects", and the discoveries "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals". (C32, C33)

## 4. The published numbers

Every number is from a published source; none is from our own code. "ee" (enantiomeric excess) is the lead of one hand: % of one hand minus % of the other. 0% is an even mix, 100% is one hand only ([SCI] footnote 1).

| What | Numbers | Source | Claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kagan's teaching example (popular text) | ligand 75% right, 25% left; catalysts 56% / 38% / 6%; working catalysts 90% right, 10% left | [POP] p. 4, figure 4 | C15 |
| The same example in ee (our arithmetic) | 75:25 is 50% ee; 90:10 is 80% ee | definition in [SCI] footnote 1 | C15 |
| Soai 1990, the copier whose lead shrinks | catalyst 86% ee, product 35% ee | [SCI] pp. 4, 11 | C18 |
| Soai 2003, three runs from a tiny head start | 0.00005% -> 57% -> 99% -> >99.5% ee | [SCI] p. 12 and figure 7; [POP] figure 6; [KS11] p. 2 | C21 |
| Soai 2003, no chiral substance added | 37 runs: 19 gave one hand, 18 the other; ee from 15% to 91% | [SCI] p. 13; [SR19] | C24 |
| Singleton and Vo 2003, independent check | 54 runs: 27 R, 27 S; about 60,000 molecules enough to decide the outcome | [SV03] | C25 |

The 1995 series is not in this table on purpose. The sources disagree (section 6), so we use the 2003 numbers everywhere.

## 5. What we will not say

- **"99.99%."** The popular text says the winning hand "can comprise 99.99 per cent of the product" ([POP] p. 6). No paper we opened gives that number. The papers and the scientific background say more than 99.5% ee.
- **"Pure", "one hand only" or "100%" for the runs with no chiral substance added.** Those runs gave 15% to 91% ee ([SCI] p. 13). The press release's "only one of the two possible mirror images was formed" ([PR]) is a simplification we do not repeat.
- **That the 0.00005% start happened by chance.** It was the starting catalyst the chemists put in (C22). The chance-driven runs are the separate 37-run experiment (C24).
- **That 1995 was Soai's first asymmetric autocatalysis.** That was 1990, and its lead of one hand shrank. 1995 was the first with amplification (C18, C19).
- **That a toy model reproduces the Soai reaction**, its numbers or its mechanism. Our models are educational sketches with fitted, made-up parameters.
- **That this explains the origin of life.** The committee says Frank's model "is not an answer to the origin of biological homochirality" and the Soai reaction "is not relevant for the emergence of biological homochirality in aqueous systems" ([SCI] pp. 4, 13). The popular text calls the reaction "artificial, different to the chemistry of life" ([POP] p. 6).
- **Any lab procedure, reagent, amount, temperature, time or handling step.** This is a safety rule. We stay at textbook level: "a zinc reagent adds to an aldehyde" is as far as we go.
- **That a one-handed thalidomide would have been safe.** The popular text says "it was the active substance's mirror image that caused the harm" ([POP] p. 4), and we quote only that. A 1995 study in people found "rapid interconversion" of the two forms in the body ([TH95]). We make no safety claim about any drug (C34, C35).
- **That the mechanism is solved**, or that one group's model is right. The committee describes two detailed mechanisms that differ (C30, C31).
- **The Merck, Noyori and quartz examples, or Kagan's age rank among laureates.** They are sourced in our research notes but not needed, so they are not in the ledger.

## 6. Where the sources disagree

- **The 1995 series (C20).** Soai's own lecture slide, citing the Nature paper, gives catalyst ee 2, 10, 57, 81, 88% and product ee 10, 57, 81, 88, 88% ([SL13] slide 7). The Nature abstract says a "small (2%)" excess ([S95]), and Soai's review says 2% "reached 88% after four consecutive" runs ([SR19]). The scientific background's figure 7, for the same unsubstituted pyrimidyl alcohol, gives 5 -> 55 -> 87 -> 88 -> 90 -> 90, and its text says "from 5% to 90%" ([SCI] pp. 11-12). The popular text says 2% to 87% ([POP] p. 5). We do not put any 1995 series on screen.
- **The 630,000 (C23).** Soai's papers say the amount of the major hand multiplied about 630,000 times over the three runs, while the minor hand multiplied less than 1,000 times ([KS11] p. 2; [SR19]). The scientific background calls it "an amplification of the stereoisomeric ratio by a factor of 630,000" ([SCI] p. 12). The ratio of the two hands goes from about 1 to more than 399, so the ratio itself grew a few hundred times, not 630,000 (our arithmetic). If we use the number, we use Soai's meaning: the amount.
- **The 2003 figure in the popular text.** [POP] figure 6 says the reaction "starts with a non-chiral solution" and, in the same panel, shows the 0.00005% -> >99.5% runs, which started from an added catalyst. We keep the two experiments apart.
- **What 1990 was.** [POP] p. 5 says that in the early 1990s "none" of the known autocatalytic reactions "were asymmetric". [SCI] pp. 4, 11 credits Soai with an asymmetric autocatalysis in 1990, without amplification. We follow [SCI].
- **Frank's three conditions.** [POP] p. 3 lists a chiral catalyst, enhancing one hand while dampening the other, and autocatalysis. [SCI] p. 3 lists autocatalysis, enantioselective catalysis and mutual antagonism. Same idea, different order; we use [SCI]'s words.
- **The award's wording.** The citation ([PR]) and [SCI] p. 2 phrase it differently (section 1).
- **The heterochiral catalyst.** [POP] says it "barely works" and runs "far more slowly". [SCI] says it is less reactive in the +NLE case, more reactive in the -NLE case, and fully inactive in the "reservoir" case. Both are right for different reactions.
